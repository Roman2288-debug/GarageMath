WHEEL FITMENT GUIDE

Wheel offset & backspacing explained

Offset and width work together. Comparing offset alone can hide the larger change in where the wheel actually sits.

Geometry explainedWorked examplesPhysical checks included

What offset measures

Wheel offset is the distance from the wheel centerline to the hub-mounting face, usually expressed in millimeters. Positive offset places the mounting face farther toward the wheel’s outer face and generally pulls the wheel inward. Lower positive offset or negative offset generally moves the wheel outward.

That description is incomplete unless wheel width stays the same. When width changes, both the inner and outer edges move relative to the centerline.

Why wheel width matters

Adding one inch of nominal wheel width adds 25.4 mm total. Half of that, 12.7 mm, appears on each side of the wheel centerline before offset is considered. Then the offset shift moves the entire wheel inward or outward.

inner position = wheel width ÷ 2 + offset
outer position = wheel width ÷ 2 − offset

Use the same units for both terms. GarageMath converts wheel width from inches to millimeters before comparing positions.

Worked example: 8-inch ET40 to 9-inch ET35

The new wheel is one inch wider, so it grows 12.7 mm on each side of the centerline. Offset drops from +40 to +35, shifting the wheel 5 mm outward. Combined, the inner edge moves about 7.7 mm closer to the suspension while the outer edge extends about 17.7 mm farther toward the fender. Looking only at the 5 mm offset change would badly understate the fitment change.

How backspacing relates

Backspacing measures from the hub-mounting face to the innermost physical edge of the wheel. Offset is centerline-based. A nominal conversion can be estimated from wheel width and offset, but nominal wheel width is measured between bead seats rather than outside lip to outside lip. That is why GarageMath can show both a nominal figure and a rough lip-adjusted figure.

nominal backspacing ≈ wheel width ÷ 2 + offset in inches

If the exact backspacing matters, use the wheel manufacturer’s measured specification or measure the wheel itself with a straightedge across the inner lip.

The tire can become the limiting part

Wheel geometry does not automatically tell you tire clearance. A tire’s section width, shoulder shape and recommended wheel-width range determine where the rubber sits relative to the wheel. Two tires with the same printed size can have different published dimensions.

Brake and spoke clearance are different problems

Offset and backspacing describe where the barrel and edges sit, but spoke shape determines caliper clearance. A wheel can have apparently suitable offset and backspacing and still contact a brake caliper. Hub bore, lug-seat design, load rating and bolt pattern are separate checks too.

A practical fitment workflow

  1. Confirm bolt pattern, hub bore, load rating and lug-seat type.
  2. Record current wheel width and offset.
  3. Compare the proposed wheel’s inner and outer position.
  4. Check the exact tire’s published section width and approved wheel-width range.
  5. Measure current suspension and fender clearance.
  6. Check steering lock and suspension compression where applicable.
  7. Verify spoke-to-caliper clearance with a manufacturer template or test fit when possible.
A few millimeters on paper can disappear quickly once tire bulge, alignment, steering angle and suspension movement are added.

What a spacer does

A spacer moves the wheel outward by approximately the spacer thickness, effectively reducing positive offset by the same amount. A 5 mm spacer on a +40 wheel produces roughly the same wheel-center position as +35, but hardware engagement, hub centering and legal or manufacturer requirements still need separate attention.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing offset numbers without accounting for width.
  • Treating nominal wheel width as outside-to-outside width.
  • Assuming offset predicts spoke-to-caliper clearance.
  • Ignoring the mounted tire’s actual section width.
  • Checking parked clearance but not steering or suspension travel.

Reference notes

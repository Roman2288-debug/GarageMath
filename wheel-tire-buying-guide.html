A measurement-first wheel & tire buying workflow

Go from a proposed wheel and tire package to a documented comparison: baseline dimensions, geometry changes, clearance measurements and final product checks.

By GarageMathUpdated October 9, 2026Illustrative measurements, no vehicle-specific fitment claims
On this page1. Record what is installed2. Separate tire diameter from wheel position3. Work through a clearance budget4. Match your measurements to the model5. Verify the exact products6. Keep a decision record

1. Record what is installed

Start with the actual vehicle, not a photo of a similar build. Write down the current tire code, wheel bead-seat width and offset. Record front and rear separately if they differ. Wheel diameter does not tell you width, and a tire code does not tell you the wheel’s offset.

Also note suspension changes, alignment settings and whether the current setup already rubs. A comparison against a baseline with an existing problem cannot establish that the replacement resolves it. The calculator needs dimensions; the decision also needs the physical context.

2. Separate tire diameter from wheel position

Use the Setup Planner to compare the complete package. Diameter affects nominal ride height, revolutions per mile and speed calibration. Wheel width and offset determine how its inner and outer edges move relative to the hub face. These are connected decisions but different measurements.

A proposed wheel can reduce inner clearance even when its offset moves the centerline outward: added width may exceed the outward shift. Read both edge changes. A small tire-diameter difference also does not establish that the wider section clears the vehicle.

3. Work through a clearance budget

Consider an illustrative move from 17×8 ET40 with 225/45R17 to 18×9 ET35 with 245/40R18. This is a mathematical example, not a recommendation for a particular car.

If measured inner wheel-edge clearance is 12 mm, 4.3 mm remains after the geometric change. If measured outer wheel-edge clearance is 15 mm, the example wheel passes that boundary by 2.7 mm. A negative result is a conflict with that measured reference. A positive result is still only a dimension, because movement and tire shape have not been modeled.

4. Match your measurements to the model

Keep wheel-edge and tire-sidewall measurements separate. A tire can project beyond the wheel. The actual mounted section width and shoulder shape matter, so a wheel-edge gap cannot be used as a tire-clearance gap.

Measure the relevant inner obstacle, outer body boundary and vertical room. Verify steering lock and suspension movement through an appropriate inspection or professional test fit. Static measurements at one ride height do not capture every operating position. Brake-spoke clearance is another independent check: equal width and offset do not mean equal spoke shape.

5. Verify the exact products

Before ordering, compare the exact tire model’s published dimensions, load and speed ratings, and approved mounting-width range. Check the exact wheel’s bolt pattern, hub bore, load rating, lug-seat type and brake-clearance information. If a seller says “fits,” establish which trim, brake package and suspension configuration that claim covers.

For AWD or staggered packages, use vehicle-manufacturer requirements for circumference and approved combinations. The planner does not invent a universal percentage tolerance. Product-specific limits outrank a convenient rule of thumb.

6. Keep a decision record

Save the comparison link or print the result. Attach product identifiers and your measured clearances. List unanswered questions explicitly, such as caliper clearance or tire-to-fender clearance at compression. This gives a shop a concrete comparison to verify and helps avoid mixing dimensions from different products.

Use the calculation to narrow the choice, then use manufacturer data and a physical check to finish it. If one critical measurement remains unknown, the result is a research starting point.

Reference and calculation basis

Wheel position is direct half-width and offset geometry. Tire calculations use nominal size-code dimensions. Consult Michelin’s tire-size guidance for vehicle-specification checks and GarageMath methodology for unit and formula standards.

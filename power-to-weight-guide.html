Power-to-weight explained

Power-to-weight is a fast way to compare how much mass each horsepower has to move, but it is not a complete acceleration model.

lb/hphp/tonComparison rules

Two common ways to express the ratio

pounds per horsepower = vehicle weight ÷ horsepower
horsepower per ton = horsepower ÷ (weight ÷ 2,000)

Lower lb/hp means less mass for each horsepower to accelerate. Higher hp/ton expresses the same relationship from the opposite direction.

Worked example

A 3,000-pound car with 300 horsepower has 10 lb/hp. A 4,000-pound car with 400 horsepower also has 10 lb/hp. Their ratios are identical, but that does not guarantee identical acceleration because tires, gearing, aerodynamics, torque delivery and transmission behavior can differ.

Which weight should you use?

Use a consistent convention. Curb weight may exclude the driver and cargo, while race weight should include the driver, fuel and equipment carried during the pass. If you compare one car at curb weight and another with driver and fuel, the ratio is biased before the horsepower is considered.

Crank horsepower versus wheel horsepower

Advertised engine horsepower and chassis-dyno wheel horsepower are not interchangeable. Wheel figures include drivetrain losses and measurement conditions that advertised crank figures do not. The calculator will accept either, but comparisons only make sense when both vehicles use the same basis.

What the ratio leaves out

Power-to-weight does not include traction, tire compound, gearing, launch strategy, torque curve, shift speed, aerodynamic drag, frontal area, downforce or thermal limits. At low speed, traction and gearing can dominate. At high speed, aerodynamic drag becomes increasingly important.

Using the ratio for modifications

The ratio is useful for comparing a realistic power gain with a realistic weight reduction. A 3,600-pound car at 400 hp is 9.0 lb/hp. Adding 40 hp gives 8.18 lb/hp. Removing 200 pounds without changing power gives 8.5 lb/hp. That does not tell you which modification is cheaper or better, but it puts the magnitude of each change on the same scale.

Relationship to quarter-mile estimates

Simple quarter-mile equations often use weight and horsepower because those two variables capture a large part of the performance picture. They are still empirical shortcuts. A poor launch can hurt elapsed time without reducing the underlying power-to-weight ratio. Trap speed can also move with aero, weather and power delivery.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing crank horsepower with wheel horsepower.
  • Using curb weight for one car and race weight for another.
  • Treating equal ratios as proof of equal performance.
  • Ignoring traction and gearing at low speed.
  • Assuming a dyno number from one shop is directly comparable to another dyno and correction method.

Verification note

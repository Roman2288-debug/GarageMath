Gear ratio, RPM & tire diameter

Road speed is the result of engine speed passing through the selected transmission gear, the final drive and the tire’s rolling diameter.

Formula shownCruise exampleSlip explained

The three ratios that matter

The transmission gear ratio multiplies engine rotation before it reaches the final drive. The axle or final-drive ratio multiplies it again. Tire diameter converts wheel rotation into distance traveled. A numerically higher overall ratio raises engine RPM at the same road speed, while a taller tire lowers RPM at the same speed.

overall ratio = transmission gear ratio × final-drive ratio

Theoretical road-speed formula

MPH ≈ RPM × tire diameter ÷ (gear ratio × final drive × 336)

The constant 336 combines inches, miles and minutes into a convenient approximation. The same relationship can be rearranged to solve for RPM at a selected road speed.

RPM ≈ MPH × gear ratio × final drive × 336 ÷ tire diameter

Worked example: 70 mph cruise

With a 26-inch tire, 0.75:1 top gear and 3.73:1 final drive, theoretical engine speed at 70 mph is about 2,530 RPM. Change only the final drive to 4.10:1 and the estimate rises to roughly 2,780 RPM. The shorter axle gear increases engine speed at the same road speed.

Direct drive and overdrive

A 1.00:1 transmission gear is direct drive. A ratio below 1.00, such as 0.75:1, is overdrive because the output turns faster than the input. A six-speed transmission is not automatically “0.6”; you need the ratio of the actual gear selected.

Tire diameter changes gearing too

A taller tire effectively lengthens gearing because the vehicle travels farther per wheel revolution. A shorter tire effectively shortens gearing. That is why a tire change can alter cruise RPM, acceleration feel and speedometer calibration without changing the differential.

Why measured RPM can differ

The formula describes a locked mechanical relationship. Real vehicles can add error through torque-converter slip, clutch slip, tire deflection, tire growth at speed, tachometer error and the difference between nominal and loaded tire diameter. An automatic transmission with an unlocked converter can show noticeably more engine RPM than the theoretical value.

Using gearing math to compare changes

Gearing math is most useful for comparing two combinations under the same assumptions. If you are choosing between axle ratios, calculate the expected RPM in the exact cruising gear you use. If you are changing tire diameter at the same time, include that change in the comparison instead of evaluating the axle ratio alone.

ChangeEffect at same road speed
Higher numerical final driveHigher engine RPM
Lower numerical final driveLower engine RPM
Taller tireLower engine RPM
Shorter tireHigher engine RPM
Lower overdrive ratioLower engine RPM

Common mistakes

  • Entering the number of transmission gears instead of the selected gear ratio.
  • Using tire sidewall code without converting it to diameter.
  • Ignoring torque-converter slip when comparing to a tachometer reading.
  • Comparing axle ratios while changing tire diameter at the same time.
  • Assuming lower cruise RPM automatically guarantees better fuel economy.

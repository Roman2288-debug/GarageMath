Reading power-to-weight

Lower pounds per horsepower means each horsepower has less vehicle mass to accelerate. Horsepower per ton expresses the same relationship from the opposite direction.

Why the ratio is not a lap-time guarantee

Traction, gearing, aerodynamics, torque curve, transmission behavior and driver inputs all matter. Power-to-weight is best treated as a quick comparison metric, not a full performance simulation.

Worked example: compare two cars consistently

A 3,000-pound car with 300 horsepower has 10 pounds per horsepower. A 4,000-pound car with 400 horsepower has the same ratio. That does not mean their launches or lap times will match: tire grip, gearing, torque delivery and aerodynamic drag can differ substantially. Include passengers, fuel and cargo in weight when comparing real driving conditions.

Know which horsepower you entered

Manufacturers usually advertise engine output, while chassis dynamometers estimate output at the wheels. Wheel and crank figures are not interchangeable. If one car is listed at the crank and another at the wheels, the comparison is tilted before any math begins. Use a consistent basis and treat the ratio as a screening tool. When planning a modification, compare how much the ratio changes after a realistic power gain or weight reduction, then consider the cost and tradeoffs separately.

Should I enter crank or wheel horsepower?

Use the same basis when comparing vehicles. Wheel horsepower accounts for drivetrain loss, while advertised crank horsepower does not; mixing them distorts the comparison.

Tire size explained

Metric tire codes pack width, sidewall proportion and wheel diameter into one string. The code is useful, but it does not tell the whole fitment story.

Reviewed Sep 2026Worked examplesFitment limits explained

How to read 225/45R17

In 225/45R17, 225 is the nominal section width in millimeters, 45 is the aspect ratio, and 17 is the wheel diameter in inches. The sidewall height is not 45 millimeters. It is 45 percent of 225 millimeters.

sidewall height = 225 × 0.45 = 101.25 mm
sidewall in inches = 101.25 ÷ 25.4 ≈ 3.99 in

The wheel contributes 17 inches to overall diameter, while the tire contributes one sidewall above and one below the wheel.

Overall diameter formula

diameter = wheel diameter + 2 × (section width × aspect ratio ÷ 100 ÷ 25.4)

Using the example above, overall diameter is about 24.97 inches. The calculation is geometric. Real tires with the same printed size can differ because manufacturers use different constructions and tread designs, and because measured dimensions depend on the wheel width used for the published specification.

Why tire diameter changes indicated speed

A taller tire travels farther per wheel revolution. If the vehicle still assumes the original rolling distance, actual road speed rises relative to the indicated speed. A shorter tire does the opposite.

actual speed = indicated speed × (new diameter ÷ old diameter)

If a 24.97-inch tire is replaced with a 25.72-inch tire, the new tire is roughly 3% taller. At an indicated 60 mph, the geometric estimate is about 61.8 mph if the original speedometer calibration was accurate.

Ride height and gearing

Ride height changes by approximately half the difference in tire diameter. A tire that is 0.75 inch taller raises the axle centerline by about 0.375 inch. The same diameter change also alters effective gearing because each revolution covers a different distance.

Why width is a separate problem

Diameter answers only one part of fitment. A wider tire can contact a strut, control arm, fender liner or body even when the diameter barely changes. Actual section width depends on the tire model and the wheel width it is mounted on. Shoulder shape matters too.

AWD and wheel-speed systems

Vehicles that compare wheel speeds can be sensitive to differences in rolling circumference. The allowable mismatch is vehicle-specific, so a generic percentage should not be treated as universal. Check the owner’s manual or manufacturer guidance before mixing significantly different tire diameters, especially on all-wheel-drive systems.

A practical comparison workflow

  1. Start with the vehicle placard, owner’s manual or manufacturer-approved size.
  2. Use the calculator to compare nominal diameter and speedometer change.
  3. Check the exact tire model’s published overall diameter, section width, load rating and approved wheel-width range.
  4. Measure suspension and body clearance on the vehicle.
  5. Check steering lock and suspension compression, not just parked clearance.
  6. Verify load capacity, speed rating and any vehicle-specific requirements before ordering.
Use nominal tire math to narrow the choices. Use manufacturer dimensions and physical measurements to make the final decision.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the aspect ratio as a millimeter measurement.
  • Assuming two brands with the same sidewall code measure exactly the same.
  • Checking diameter but ignoring width and wheel offset.
  • Assuming the original speedometer was perfectly accurate.
  • Using an internet “3% rule” as a universal fitment or AWD specification.

Worked comparison: 225/45R17 vs 245/40R18

The 225/45R17 works out to roughly 24.97 inches in nominal diameter. The 245/40R18 is roughly 25.72 inches. That is about a 3% increase in diameter, about 0.375 inch of ride-height increase, and roughly 61.8 mph actual road speed at 60 mph indicated under the calculator assumptions. The wider replacement also needs a separate wheel-fitment and clearance check.
